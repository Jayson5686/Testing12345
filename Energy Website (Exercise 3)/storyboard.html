This page sets out the data story for television energy use: who it is for, why it is being told, and the consumer questions the KNIME charts are meant to answer. Open one section at a time, the same way as the FAQ on Home.

The main target audience for this data story is consumers who are interested in purchasing a television with lower energy consumption. These consumers may want to understand whether factors such as screen size, television model, and energy-efficiency rating have a noticeable effect on how much electricity a television uses.

Many buyers usually compare features such as display size, picture quality, brand, and price, but energy consumption may also affect the long-term cost of owning a television. This data story is therefore designed to make energy-related information easier to understand by presenting comparisons between different televisions.

The findings may also be useful to policy makers, regulators, and researchers who are interested in general patterns of energy efficiency in consumer electronics.

The purpose of this data story is to investigate how different television characteristics are related to energy consumption and to present the results in a way that is useful for consumers. The story will examine factors such as screen size, energy-efficiency rating, and differences between television models to identify patterns in energy usage.

For example, it will explore whether larger televisions tend to consume more energy, whether televisions with better efficiency ratings generally use less electricity, and whether televisions with similar specifications can still have different levels of energy consumption.

By using comparisons and visualisations, the story aims to help consumers recognise which factors may have a greater influence on energy use and encourage them to consider energy efficiency alongside other features when choosing a television.

Does choosing a larger TV increase energy consumption?

As shown in the chart, large televisions have the highest average energy consumption at approximately 741.30 kWh/year, compared with 402.73 kWh/year for medium televisions and 157.98 kWh/year for small televisions. This shows a clear increase in average energy consumption across the three screen-size groups, suggesting that screen size is an important factor for consumers to consider when choosing an energy-efficient television.

Does a better energy rating generally mean lower energy consumption?

As shown in the scatter plot, televisions with higher energy ratings generally have lower labelled energy consumption. In contrast, televisions with lower star ratings are more likely to have higher energy usage. Although the points are spread out within each rating, the overall pattern suggests that energy rating is an important indicator of how much energy a television consumes.

For TVs of similar sizes, does screen technology affect average energy consumption?

As shown in the chart, TVs of the same size can still have different levels of energy consumption depending on their screen technology. LCD models have the lowest average energy consumption across the three size groups in this dataset, while the difference between LCD (LED) and OLED changes depending on the TV size. This means that screen technology can make a difference, but consumers should consider it together with screen size rather than looking at the technology alone.

The data story shows that screen size, energy rating, and screen technology are all important factors that can affect how much energy a television uses. Consumers who want to reduce their electricity usage should consider these factors when choosing a television.

In general, larger televisions tend to use more energy, while televisions with higher energy ratings tend to use less. Screen technology can also make a difference, but it is best considered together with screen size rather than in isolation.
